1. Executive decision and proposed business line

This is an adjacent business opportunity for consideration, not a description of 3DS Capital’s current business. The proposed venture would help U.S. property-management companies delegate selected, repeatable back-office workflows to trained offshore specialists while retaining control of their systems, approvals, data and service standards. The commercial thesis is that 3DS Capital could help originate client relationships and develop the venture, provided an experienced delivery structure is put in place.

Proposed business

Proposed adjacent venture

Develop a specialist offshore staffing business serving U.S. property-management companies. 3DS Capital could act as venture sponsor and commercial lead, while a dedicated operating lead or delivery partner manages recruitment, training, payroll, quality assurance, security coordination and day-to-day client delivery.

Proposed positioning: Dedicated offshore property-operations support with clear accountability, controlled access and predictable delivery.

Operating boundary: 3DS Capital’s role is proposed, not presumed. It may sponsor the venture and lead client acquisition; the delivery operator may be 3DS Capital itself or a contracted operating partner, depending on capability, structure and the founder’s decision.

2. Market opportunity and positioning

Established providers in the offshore staffing and property-operations support market illustrate two broad patterns. Managed providers bundle recruiting, training, workforce support, security controls and performance oversight. Marketplace or client-managed arrangements give the client more direct control of hiring, access and day-to-day supervision, with optional support services. These are useful reference models, not proof that all U.S. companies accept offshore access from Nigeria.

Managed-service reference

Revaya-style positioning

  • Dedicated staffing and property-management workflow familiarity.
  • Structured onboarding, HR/performance support and documented controls.
  • Potentially higher fee because the provider takes on more coordination and oversight.
  • Venture lesson: only promise controls and replacement support that are implemented and budgeted.
Client-managed reference

Marketplace / direct-management positioning

  • Client retains direct daily management and often sets permissions and workflow priorities.
  • Provider focuses on sourcing, screening, payment or workforce tools.
  • Lower ongoing provider workload, but more work and risk remain with the client.
  • Venture lesson: clearly state who supervises, approves work, manages access and handles incidents.
Competitive boundary: any market pricing discussed during planning is indicative and may change. The proposed venture should not rely on competitor price claims until current public pricing and package inclusions are rechecked. The proposed prices in this document are internal planning hypotheses, not a verified market rate.

3. Two service models: managed versus client-managed

These offers must not be sold as the same service at different prices. The client-managed model reduces the delivery operator’s day-to-day supervision burden but should also reduce the promises it makes. It does not remove the need for confidentiality, secure access, lawful contracting or a clear incident process.

Model A · Recommended core offer

Managed specialist

The delivery operator owns: recruiting and screening, role-fit checks, onboarding, training, defined work standards, routine QA, attendance coordination, performance check-ins, security-policy administration within agreed scope, escalation tracking and documented handover.

Client owns: business priorities, system and data authorization, final decisions, approvals, payment release, tenant/owner policy decisions, legal determinations and any licensed activity.

Best for: clients who want the specialist to be productive without having to manage every administrative detail themselves.

One-time placement and onboarding fee: $1,200 per successful placement as the proposed launch price. Covers recruitment, screening, client selection support, role-specific onboarding, workflow documentation and initial quality checks.

Monthly managed service fee: $1,850–$2,000 for a full-time 160-hour placement, subject to cost and demand validation.

Model B · Lower-touch offer

Client-managed specialist

The delivery operator owns: sourcing, screening, placement, onboarding, payroll and specialist payments, timesheet logging and submission for client approval, payment administration, a monthly quality scorecard, and defined payroll/attendance issue resolution.

Client owns: daily supervision, task assignment, training on its internal processes, day-to-day work review, timely approval or dispute of submitted timesheets, system permissions and business/operational decisions. The delivery operator pays the specialist in both models under the agreed payroll schedule, using the approved hours and documented dispute process.

Best for: experienced clients with documented SOPs, an internal supervisor and the capacity to manage a remote team member.

One-time placement fee: $950 per successful placement as the proposed launch price. Covers sourcing, screening, candidate introduction, client selection support and basic onboarding.

Monthly client-managed service fee: proposed at $1,750 per full-time placement. This includes payroll administration and payment, timesheet logging and submission for client approval before payroll processing, a monthly quality scorecard, and defined administrative support. The client manages day-to-day work; the delivery operator still administers and pays its specialists in both service models.

Unmanaged does not mean uncontrolled

Even where the client supervises the specialist, the delivery operator should maintain a minimum standard: signed confidentiality terms, documented scope, a defined access/incident escalation procedure, proof of onboarding and a clean offboarding checklist. The distinction is who manages day-to-day output and system administration, not whether basic safeguards exist.

Recommended price guardrails

  • Do not discount the managed service below fully loaded direct cost plus a contribution sufficient to fund central operations.
  • Set a minimum monthly commitment or scope cap; open-ended work creates unpriced supervision.
  • Quote part-time roles based on actual hours and minimum delivery costs, not a straight fraction of full-time pricing.
  • Charge separately for recruiting rushes, bespoke software training, additional QA, equipment shipping, after-hours coverage or dedicated backup where applicable.
  • Review pricing quarterly during the first year as actual costs and retention data emerge.
  • Keep the client-managed fee within about $150 of the managed fee. A wider gap erodes contribution faster than the supervision it saves.
  • Model any sponsor or operating-lead payment in the break-even count before it is agreed.

7. Operating model: from lead to stable delivery

Step 1 · Client qualification

Confirm portfolio type and size, software, recurring task volume, U.S. business-hour overlap, data categories, offshore-access policy, existing SOPs, supervisor availability, security requirements, budget and success measures. Reject opportunities that require prohibited access or unclear responsibility.

Step 2 · Scope and proposal

Define role outcomes, hours, working schedule, task queue, communication channels, turnaround times, acceptance criteria, exclusions, escalation levels, equipment owner, system owner, pricing, payment timing, term, termination and replacement terms.

Step 3 · Candidate recruitment and assessment

Screen written communication, spreadsheet accuracy, attention to detail, workflow judgment, confidentiality awareness, reliability and role-specific software familiarity. Use a practical test with fictional or sanitized data. Verify references and identity only through lawful, proportionate processes.

Step 4 · Contracting and security approval

Complete the client vendor/security review, contract and confidentiality documents, data-processing terms where applicable, device choice, account request, access matrix, approved location and incident contacts before any production access.

Step 5 · Onboarding and controlled pilot

Train on the client's SOPs, use sandbox or redacted records where possible, run a limited task batch, review errors daily during the initial period and document client sign-off before expanding the task scope.

Step 6 · Business-as-usual delivery

Daily task queue and exception log; weekly client update; regular sample-based QA; monthly KPI review; access review after role changes; documented coaching and retraining when issues recur.

Step 7 · Renewal, expansion or offboarding

Review workload, quality, value, security changes and economics. Expand permissions only with client approval. On exit, transfer open work, return assets, revoke access and confirm data disposition.

12. Assumptions, definitions and validation notes

Definitions

  • Managed specialist: the delivery operator provides defined ongoing oversight and quality controls in addition to staffing. Client retains business authority and system authorization.
  • Client-managed specialist: client handles daily task management and internal workflow training; the delivery operator still administers payroll, pays the specialist, logs and submits timesheets for client approval, and provides the agreed quality scorecard.
  • Direct delivery costs: costs that vary with a placement, such as equipment allocation, security tools, onboarding, QA and payment administration.
  • Central overhead: shared costs such as general administration, marketing, legal/accounting, management and business systems.
  • Contribution before central overhead: client revenue minus specialist compensation and direct delivery costs. It is not net profit.
  • Residual / surplus: contribution less the assumed central overhead; still not net profit if other costs or founder compensation are omitted.
